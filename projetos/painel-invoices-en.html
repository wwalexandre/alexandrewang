Import invoice tracker

An import invoice arrives by email, becomes an attachment lost in an inbox and only resurfaces when someone asks where the cargo is. This tracker brings them all into one list and answers three questions: what stage each one is at, which one is late and what the Incoterm changes in the follow-up.

The problem

In an import operation, the invoice is the document that starts everything: it says what was bought, for how much and on what delivery terms. The problem is the path it takes. It arrives as an email attachment, each analyst keeps their own in a folder, and the follow-up becomes a spreadsheet someone updates when they remember.

The result is familiar: nobody can quickly say how many invoices are on the way, which one is past its deadline and who should be chasing the carrier.

How the flow works

The idea is to take the invoice out of email the moment it arrives, without depending on someone saving the attachment:

  1. The email arrives in Outlook. A rule recognises invoice messages by sender and subject.
  2. Power Automate saves the attachment. The PDF goes to a SharePoint folder organised by year and month, with the invoice number in the file name.
  3. The main data becomes a row. Number, supplier, Incoterm, value and dates go into a single list. What the flow cannot read is flagged for manual checking.
  4. The tracker reads the list. Each invoice gets a stage and an expected arrival, and the late ones rise to the top.

The layout follows the logic of a tracking app: the numbers on top, the overall status in one bar and the list below, with each invoice's detail one click away.

What the Incoterm changes in the follow-up

The three letters on the invoice define who contracts the freight and at what point the risk passes from seller to buyer. That changes who has to be chased at each stage:

IncotermWho contracts the main freightWhere the risk passesWhat to track
EXWBuyerAt the seller's premises, before loading.Pickup at origin and export clearance, which are also on the buyer.
FCABuyerOn handover to the carrier named by the buyer.The handover to the carrier and the export clearance, which is the seller's.
FOBBuyerWhen the goods are on board the vessel.The on-board date on the bill of lading. Sea only.
CIFSellerOn board at the port of shipment, although the seller pays freight and insurance.The bill of lading and the insurance policy. Sea only.
DAPSellerAt the named place of destination, ready for unloading.Arrival at destination. Import clearance stays with the buyer.

The detail that misleads most is in CIF: the seller pays freight to destination, but the risk already passed to the buyer at shipment. Anyone reading only "freight paid" assumes the cargo is protected until it arrives.

What the tracker answers

  • How many invoices are open and what they are worth: the numbers on top, which change with the filters.
  • Where the build-up is: the status bar shows whether volume is stuck before shipment, in transit or in customs.
  • What to chase today: the list is ordered by expected arrival, with late ones flagged and the days of delay.
  • Who to chase: each invoice's detail says who contracts the freight and what that Incoterm requires.

About this page: it is a demo built in HTML and JavaScript, with 30 fictional invoices and suppliers generated in the browser. The flow described uses Outlook, Power Automate and SharePoint. No company data was used.

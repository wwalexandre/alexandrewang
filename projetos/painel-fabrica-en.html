Capacity and coverage of a confectionery plant

Three questions decide what the plant does next month: how much of the lines is occupied, where time is lost and whether stock is healthy. This dashboard answers all three with fictional data from a confectionery plant, with three lines and eight items.

The problem

When the plant asks for a quick diagnosis, what reaches the table is usually a single number: occupancy. That number hides three different questions. How much of the line is really occupied? What are the leftover hours, or the missing ones, being spent on? And is the stock leaving that line the right size?

The dashboard brings the three answers onto one screen, with the same month and line filters for every chart.

What the dashboard answers

  • Where the hours go: each line's available hours are split between production, setup, stops and idle time. Each line's occupancy appears beside its name.
  • Who is close to the cap: occupancy by line and month, with the 85% cap dashed. A line that climbs every month and passes the cap is a bottleneck candidate.
  • Where the loss is: the Pareto orders stop reasons by hours and shows the cumulative percentage. A few reasons usually explain most of the lost hours.
  • Which items are out of range: each item's coverage in multiples of the target. Below 0.5x is stockout risk, above 1.5x is excess.

A definition that changes the number

Occupancy is occupied hours divided by available hours, but what counts as an occupied hour is a choice. If stops are included, the line looks fuller. If they are left out, the number shows only the time the line produced or prepared to produce.

The "Do stops count as occupancy?" switch toggles the two readings, and the difference between them is the size of the stops. The plant as a whole is not the average of its lines: the calculation uses the sum of occupied hours divided by the sum of available hours, and the lines have different hours. Line 2, for example, runs 2 shifts, and the other two run 3.

How the dashboard was built

The hours are generated shift by shift, with a fixed seed, so every visitor sees the same numbers. Each shift has 8 hours, with a few of 4, and gets setup and stops drawn within ranges per line. Stop reasons have different weights on each line. Stock has a start-of-month level, the month's sales and a target coverage per item, and coverage is the opening stock divided by the month's average daily sales.

What the dashboard shows with the example data

  • The average hides the lines. The plant closes the quarter at 85.5% occupancy, but line 1 is at 76.9%, line 2 at 87.7% and line 3 at 92.6%.
  • Two lines pass the 85% cap in all three months. Line 3 is the one that climbs every month, from 89.3% in April to 93.2% in May and 95.3% in June. Line 2 runs only 2 shifts, so a third shift is a possible lever.
  • Stops add up to 318 h, 8.3% of available hours. Breakdowns, material shortages and corrective maintenance explain 86% of them. On line 3, breakdowns and corrective maintenance together account for 69.5 h.
  • Without counting stops, plant occupancy falls from 85.5% to 77.2%. The difference is the size of the stops.
  • The bottleneck starts to show in stock. In June, sour gummies are at stockout risk (0.38x the target) and mint hard candy is in excess (2.40x). The two dragée items on line 3 lose coverage from April to June.

Limits

  • Coverage uses the month's own sales. For seasonal items, it pays to read next month's forecast too, as in the publication on coverage.
  • Occupancy measures hours, not volume. Two shifts with the same occupancy can produce different quantities if throughput changes.
  • The 85% cap is a convention, applied here per line.

About this page: it is a demo built in HTML and JavaScript, with an invented plant, lines, shifts, items and numbers generated in the browser. No company data was used.

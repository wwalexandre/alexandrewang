Importing: the deadline game

An air import of healthcare products seen as a board game: each step is a square, and some mistakes send the cargo back to the start. The cases are real, told without the names of companies, products or partners.

6 min read1:40 videoOct 2026

The board

From the outside, importing looks like three steps: order, plane and stock. In practice, the cargo moves across a board with dozens of squares, and each one has an owner, a document and a deadline. A badly handled square does not just cause delays: sometimes it sends the cargo back to origin.

The most expensive cards

Surprise card: the misleading date

The invoice has a shipment date field. In many cases, that date is the day the order was loaded into the supplier's system, not the day the plane left. The actual departure is on the air waybill or in the freight forwarder's follow-up.

Planning the arrival from the invoice date gets the arrival wrong, and the error spreads: coverage, backorders and replenishment priorities all end up wrong.

Paying the freight yourself: the quote expires

When the importer pays the freight, forwarders' offers are valid for only a few days. If approval is late, the offer expires and the new quote can come back more expensive, sometimes on a different route. When comparing offers, check that all of them include pickup, export clearance and documents, and remember that air freight is charged by chargeable (volumetric) weight.

The most expensive card: a batch made before the approval

For some healthcare products, ANVISA (Brazil's health regulator) approves that specific import and only accepts batches manufactured after the approval date. In one case, a batch made before it was shipped. The cargo arrived in Brazil and had to go back.

The lesson: before approving the shipment, ask for the document with the batch number and manufacturing date of each item and compare it with the approval date. Once the plane takes off, between time zones, connections and holidays at origin, there is almost never time to cancel.

Demo products: in and back out

An unregistered product used only for demonstration enters under temporary admission and has to go back to origin. It must never travel in the same shipment as registered products. Consolidating cargo saves freight, but if one item gets held at inspection, everything else stays held with it, including what was urgent for month-end.

A creative description on the air waybill

The cargo description on the air waybill must match the documents and the inspection report from origin. Adding a brand name or changing the description to "make it easier" is usually refused at origin and can turn into a requirement on arrival.

When the cargo arrives

Landing in Guarulhos (São Paulo's international airport) does not mean the cargo is yours. It stays in the bonded warehouse until release, and every day costs money.

  • Declaration channel: green is released straight away; yellow requires document review; red also requires physical inspection. There is also a grey channel, rarer, used when there is suspicion of fraud, for example in the declared value.
  • Damage: if the warehouse reports damage, the customs broker asks for a reply, usually within 24 hours. Reply, and keep the email and photos to file the insurance claim.
  • Inbound invoice: once the cargo is released, enter the purchase order in the system with the invoice number and request the NF. Until it is issued, the cargo does not leave the airport and the daily fee keeps running.
  • Batches: on receipt, check batch, manufacturing date and expiry for each range before entering it in the system. Only then does the product become available stock.

Checklist to avoid going back to the start

  1. Documents checked: invoice, packing list, shipping list and certificate of analysis (quantities, batches, minimum shelf life and tax ID).
  2. Departure date taken from the air waybill or the follow-up, not from the invoice.
  3. Incoterm read: who books the freight and until when the quote is valid.
  4. Product that needs import approval: batch and manufacturing date compared with the approval date before approving the shipment.
  5. Unregistered products never in the same shipment as registered ones.
  6. Air waybill description identical to the documents and the inspection report.
  7. Damage answered within 24 hours, with the evidence kept.
  8. Purchase order and inbound invoice without delay after release.

In short: importing is a chain of deadlines. Every document requirement turns into storage days and stockout risk. Planning only by the supplier's lead time forgets the squares in the middle of the board.

Real cases of air imports of healthcare products, told without the names of companies, products, partners or processes. The video scoreboard uses fictional values.

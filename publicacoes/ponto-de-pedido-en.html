How much to order, when to order and how much to keep in reserve

High inventory and stockouts at the same time are rarely a lack of product. They are a lack of criteria for three decisions: how much to order, when to order and how much to keep in reserve. This text explains the three with a tub of whey protein, shows the formulas with a worked example and ends in a simulator. The numbers are fictional.

6 min readSimulatorNov 2026

The tub of whey

Anyone who takes whey protein every day already manages inventory without calling it that. Buying too many tubs takes up space, may expire and leaves money sitting in the cupboard. Buying too few ends with nothing left after training. And buying a little all the time means paying shipping on every order.

In a company the logic is the same, only with thousands of products. It all comes down to three questions, and each has a technical name:

QuestionIn the tub of wheyTechnical name
How much to buy?The purchase size that balances shipping against a full cupboard.Economic order quantity (EOQ)
When to buy?The level in the tub at which the order has to go out, to arrive before it runs out.Reorder point (ROP)
How much reserve?The bottom of the tub, used only when delivery is late or consumption rises.Safety stock (SS)

1. How much to order: the economic order quantity

Two costs move in opposite directions. Ordering little, many times, raises the ordering cost: freight, buyer time, receiving. Ordering a lot, few times, raises the holding cost: tied-up capital, space, risk of expiry. The economic order quantity is the order size where the sum of the two is lowest.

EOQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ H)

  • D: demand for the year, in units.
  • S: cost of placing one order.
  • H: cost of holding one unit in stock for a year.

2. How much reserve: safety stock

Demand is never equal to the average. Safety stock is the cushion that absorbs demand variation while the order is on its way.

SS = z × σ × √L

  • z: factor for the desired service level. For 95%, z = 1.65.
  • σ: standard deviation of daily demand.
  • L: lead time, in days.

The reserve grows with the square root of the lead time. Doubling the lead time does not double safety stock: it raises it by about 41%.

3. When to order: the reorder point

It is the expected consumption during the lead time, plus the reserve. When stock reaches that level, the order goes out.

ROP = d × L + SS

  • d: average demand per day.
  • L: lead time, in days.

The worked example

A product sells 50 units a day, over 300 selling days a year, with a standard deviation of 10 units a day. Each order costs R$ 50, holding one unit for a year costs R$ 6, the supplier delivers in 5 days and the desired service level is 95%.

CalculationResult
Annual demand = 50 × 30015,000 units
EOQ = √(2 × 15,000 × 50 ÷ 6)500 units
SS = 1.65 × 10 × √537 units
ROP = 50 × 5 + 37287 units

The decision fits in one sentence: when stock reaches 287, order 500. If demand follows the average, the new lot arrives with 37 units still in reserve.

Where the formula is not enough

  • The real lot is almost never the exact EOQ. Supplier minimum lot, pack multiples, storage capacity and volume discounts usually set the final number. EOQ works as a reference for what it costs to move away from it.
  • A lead time that varies needs more reserve. The formula above assumes a fixed delivery time. When it swings, its variation enters the calculation along with demand's.
  • Intermittent demand breaks the assumption. For an item that sells little and irregularly, mean and standard deviation describe consumption poorly, and safety stock calculated this way misleads.
  • Shelf life limits the lot. For a perishable or pharmaceutical product, an economic lot larger than what is consumed within shelf life becomes a loss.
  • Service level is a business decision. Going from 95% to 99% raises the reserve by about 41% in the example. The simulator shows that cost before anyone promises the level.

All numbers in this article are fictional and serve only as an example. No company data was used.

The ship sank. Who pays for the loss?

The answer is in three letters on the invoice. This is the full text behind the video I posted on LinkedIn, with a table of the 11 terms and what each one changes for planning.

6 min read1:21 videoOct 2026

The case

The cargo was loaded in China. The supplier paid the freight to Brazil. Halfway across the ocean, the ship runs into trouble and the cargo is lost.

Who takes the loss? Many people answer "the supplier, because they paid the freight". If the Incoterm on the invoice is CFR, that answer is wrong: the loss belongs to the buyer.

Cost and risk do not always travel together. Under CFR and CIF the seller pays the freight to the destination port, but risk passes to the buyer when the cargo is placed on board, still at the port of origin.

What an Incoterm is

Incoterms are rules from the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) that state, in three letters, how seller and buyer split three things:

  1. Cost: who pays each leg of transport, the insurance and the customs clearances.
  2. Risk: from which point a loss or damage becomes the buyer's problem.
  3. Tasks: who books the transport and who handles export and import formalities.

On the invoice it shows up as a code plus a place, for example CIF Santos, Incoterms 2020. The code is the rule. The place says where it applies. Without the place, the rule is incomplete.

What an Incoterm does not define: when ownership of the goods passes, the payment method and the price. Those belong in the sales contract.

The 11 terms in one table

The current version is from 2020. Seven terms work for any mode of transport and four are only for sea and inland waterway.

"No obligation" means the Incoterm does not require anyone to buy insurance. Whoever carries the risk on that leg usually does.

An easy way to remember

Group them by the first letter. The further down the alphabet, the more obligation stays with the seller:

  • E (EXW): the seller only makes the goods available.
  • F (FCA, FAS, FOB): the seller hands the goods to transport. The main freight is on the buyer.
  • C (CPT, CIP, CFR, CIF): the seller pays the main freight, but risk stays at origin. This is where the confusion lives.
  • D (DAP, DPU, DDP): the seller delivers at destination and carries the risk all the way there.

The C-term trap

With the C terms there are two different points on the same journey:

  • The risk point, which stays at origin (on board for CFR and CIF; at the first carrier for CPT and CIP).
  • The cost point, which goes all the way to destination, because the seller paid the freight.

Back to the ship: under CFR Santos, the supplier paid the freight to Santos, but the cargo was already the buyer's risk from the moment it went on board in China. If the buyer did not insure it, they lose the cargo and still have to replenish the stock.

CIF is similar, with one difference: the seller must buy insurance on the buyer's behalf. But the required cover is the minimum one. For high-value cargo, it is worth looking at additional cover.

What it changes for planners

Incoterms look like a trade compliance topic. They hit two planning numbers directly:

1. Landed cost

Under FOB, international freight and insurance go into your cost. Under CIF, they are already in the supplier's price. Comparing two quotes without looking at the Incoterm is comparing different things.

2. Lead time: where the supplier's lead time ends

The lead time a supplier quotes means something different depending on the Incoterm. Under EXW or FCA, their lead time ends when the goods are ready for pickup or handed to the carrier. Pickup, international freight, customs clearance and transport to the warehouse are steps you need to add and track. Under DAP, the supplier's lead time already runs to destination, but import clearance is still on you. Using the same lead time for suppliers on different Incoterms distorts the reorder point and the safety stock.

Example with fictional numbers

Same product, two suppliers:

Supplier B looked more expensive on price alone. With the Incoterm in the math, it is cheaper all the way to Santos. Even so, under CIF the cargo is your risk from loading, and their insurance is the minimum.

Checklist when an invoice arrives

  1. Does the Incoterm have a code, a place and a version? Example: FCA Shanghai Airport, Incoterms 2020.
  2. Does the term match the purchase order and the contract?
  3. Does the term fit the transport mode? FAS, FOB, CFR and CIF are only for sea and inland waterway. For air or containerized cargo, prefer FCA, CPT or CIP.
  4. Who bought the insurance, and what does it cover?
  5. Does the lead time in the system account for where your responsibility starts?

In short: read the Incoterm before the price. It tells you who pays each leg, from where the risk is yours and where your lead time starts.

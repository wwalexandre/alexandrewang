Is your forecast wrong? Measure before blaming sales.

Every demand review has the same argument, and almost nobody brings a number. This is the full text behind the video, with the three cuts I use to break down the error, an example and a checklist to download.

5 min read0:58 videoOct 2026

The same argument every month

The forecast missed by 40% this month. Planning says sales changed the number. Sales says the market changed. The meeting ends without a conclusion, and next month the conversation repeats itself.

The problem is not a lack of opinions. It is the lack of a number that shows where the forecast misses and who touched it. Before talking about blame, break the error down in three cuts.

The right question is not "who got it wrong". It is "where did it miss, in which direction, and did the manual override help or hurt".

1. Break the error down by product family

A 40% total error hides where the problem is. When you split it by family, a Pareto pattern almost always shows up: a few families explain most of the error.

To measure each family's weight, add up its absolute error and divide by the total absolute error:

Weight in error = sum of |actual - forecast| for the family ÷ sum of |actual - forecast| overall

A30%30%
B24%54%
C16%70%
D to H30%100%

In the example, 3 of 8 families explain 70% of the error. The meeting starts with them. The other five can wait.

For the total error, prefer the weighted error (WMAPE: sum of absolute errors divided by the sum of actuals) over a simple average of percentages. In a simple average, an item that sells 2 units and misses by 1 weighs as much as your best seller.

2. Compare statistical vs. adjusted (FVA)

Almost every demand process has two versions of the number: the statistical forecast, straight from the model, and the adjusted forecast, after manual changes by sales or planning. FVA (Forecast Value Add) measures whether that adjustment added value:

FVA = statistical forecast error - adjusted forecast error

Version (fictional)Error (WMAPE)
Statistical forecast25%
Forecast adjusted by sales32%
FVA of the adjustment-7 points

A negative FVA means the adjustment made the forecast worse. In that case, the problem is not the model. It is the adjustment process.

Three things to keep the number fair:

  • Calculate it by family and by who made the adjustment. An override can help one family and hurt another.
  • Look at least at three months in a row. A single month can be an exception.
  • Always compare on the same horizon (for example, Lag 1 or Lag 3). A forecast made one month ahead and one made three months ahead naturally have different errors.

3. Check the bias

Absolute error tells you how big the error is. Bias tells you the direction. If the error always goes the same way, it is not noise: it is a pattern.

Bias = sum of (forecast - actual) ÷ sum of actual

  • Positive bias: the forecast sits above actual demand. The result is excess stock, high coverage and expiry risk.
  • Negative bias: the forecast sits below actual demand. The result is shortages and stockouts.

In the video example, the adjusted forecast was above actual demand in 12 out of 12 months, with a +15% bias. That is not bad luck. It is an optimistic adjustment made every month, and it shows up straight in inventory.

4. Turn it into action

With the three cuts on the table, the meeting changes topic. Instead of "who got it wrong", the agenda becomes:

  1. Which families explain most of the error?
  2. Where is the manual override making the number worse?
  3. Does the error always go the same way?
  4. Who does what before the next meeting?

Each critical family leaves with an action, an owner and a deadline. Overrides with negative FVA are reviewed or dropped. And the same three numbers come back at the next meeting to show whether things improved.

In short: break the error down by family, compare statistical against adjusted and look at the direction of the error. With these three cuts, the meeting stops being about blame and becomes about action.
